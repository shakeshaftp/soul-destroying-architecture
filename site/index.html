Building Beautifully A project of the Manhattan Institute

We know which buildings people love. We keep building the other kind.

A project of the Manhattan Institute on beauty in the built environment: why it matters for wellbeing, for housing supply, and for the environment.

Paul Shakeshaft and John Ketcham

Why it matters

Beauty is not a finishing touch. It decides what gets built at all.

Wellbeing

People do not merely prefer beautiful places, they behave differently in them. A street worth walking down is walked down. And the buildings we put up outlast almost every other decision a city makes.

Housing

A good deal of what gets called NIMBYism is a reasonable objection to ugliness. Neighbours shown something worth having are far readier to accept density — which is how young and working families get a foot on the ladder.

Environment

The greenest building is the one nobody wants to demolish. Beauty earns a building the affection that keeps it standing, maintained and adapted, instead of replaced every forty years.

The comparisons

Not old against new. Loved against unloved.

The easiest way to dismiss an argument about beauty is to call it nostalgia, so this set is chosen to make that difficult. Each pair holds two buildings of the same use at a similar scale, several of the buildings we admire here are modern, and some come from well outside the Western tradition. Every pair states why it is a fair comparison.

Railway station

1 / 6

Why this is a fair comparison. The strongest control available: same city, same site, same railway, same purpose, and only the design changed. The 1910 station was demolished in 1963; the replacement concourse opened beneath Madison Square Garden in 1968.

Town hall

2 / 6

Why this is a fair comparison. Both are postwar modernist civic buildings by celebrated architects, sixteen years apart. This pair is here to argue against our own side: the problem is not modernism. Aalto's building is as modern as Boston's, and people are fond of it.

Social housing

3 / 6

Why this is a fair comparison. Both were avant-garde municipal housing schemes by architects at the top of their profession, built as showpieces of what social housing could be. One is protected and well loved by the people in it; the other was demolished while critics campaigned to save it.

Place of worship

4 / 6

Why this is a fair comparison. Both are major modern religious buildings by leading designers with national backing. Liverpool has real defenders and its interior light is genuinely admired; it is here because its fabric failed within a few years, which is a cost that arguments about beauty are usually accused of ignoring.

Railway station

5 / 6

Why this is a fair comparison. Two large stations inserted into historic Japanese cities within a decade of each other, both thoroughly modern, both generously funded. Kanazawa carries its city's tradition into new material; Kyoto was controversial before it opened and remains so.

Housing district

6 / 6

Why this is a fair comparison. Two large planned residential districts in the same country, both state-backed, both intended as models of their moment. They are roughly thirty years apart, which makes this the weakest control in the set; the note is here to say so rather than hide it.
